The metal tap is gone. Every recording is CC0 or public domain. Pick by ear; the settings box at the bottom records your choices.

What was measured

    
    
      
  • A single coin clink rings at about 4.9, 8.5, 13.2 and 17.5 kHz. The ratio of the first two, 1.73, is the textbook ratio of a free thin disc's first two modes, so the synth uses those four.
    • 
      
  • Each mode is split into two close tones (4875 and 4992 Hz in one clink). That beating is the shimmer that makes metal sound like a coin; the split control sets it.
    • 
      
  • The online coins ring differently: the small coin on the floor (Wikimedia) at 10 and 17.8 kHz, close to the textbook value for a 24 mm coin; the €2 coin on varnished wood mostly through the table, at 0.6 to 2.4 kHz; the table drop (OpenGameArt) at 0.7 to 1.1 and 3.1 to 3.8 kHz.
    • 
      
  • The €2 recording wobbles with rim contacts about 40 ms apart (25 a second) for its first half second, the same rhythm the synth wobble starts from.
    • 
            
  • A wobbling coin is an Euler's disk: its rim contact rate rises as it settles, roughly as (time left)−1/3, then stops dead. The wobble here climbs from 16 contacts a second to the end-rate control.
    • 
      
  • Why our first takes sounded so metallic: the real coins on wood put 63–85% of their energy at 1–4 kHz and almost none above 8 kHz; the Kenney clinks and the first synth put 81% at 4–8 kHz. The Kenney clips are coins striking coins in a hand. Metal on metal touches for a few microseconds, which excites every ring mode. A coin on wood stays in contact far longer, because wood gives, and a long contact cannot excite fast vibrations, so the coin's ring is barely started; the wood also drains it while the coin rests on it. What you hear is the table, ringing at 0.6–2.4 kHz. The €2 coin is also bimetallic, and the joint between its two metals damps its ring further.
    • 
      
  • The wooden-table surface (now the default) models that: the strike is softened above the contact-softness control, the table's own modes ring under it, and the blend's clinks are low-passed the same way. It measures 72% at 1–4 kHz and nothing above 8 kHz, like the recordings. The hard surface is the original bright model.
    • 
      
  • Size: a coin's ring sits at 4 to 17 kHz, but on wood almost nothing above 8 kHz survives, so recorded clinks could use the same 16 kHz MP3 as the dice clacks. The synth costs a few hundred bytes of code.
